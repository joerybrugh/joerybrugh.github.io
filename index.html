A FIELD GUIDE / LABELS, WITH CONTEXT

Many names.
No official type list.

“Empath types” is a popular way of describing different experiences—not a standardized psychological taxonomy. These labels overlap, and none can tell you what another person feels or diagnose them.

01 / EVERYDAY LANGUAGE

Emotional empath

Often used for someone who is strongly affected by other people’s emotions or responds with emotional resonance.

Research lens: affective empathy is studied, but feeling strongly does not prove you feel someone else’s exact emotion.

02 / EVERYDAY LANGUAGE

Cognitive empath

Describes trying to understand another person’s point of view, thoughts, or likely feelings.

Research lens: perspective-taking is a studied process; understanding a perspective is not the same as agreeing with it or sharing its emotion.

03 / EVERYDAY LANGUAGE

Compassionate empath

Usually means empathy paired with concern and a wish to help, while still respecting the other person’s needs and boundaries.

Research lens: compassion and empathic processes are related topics, not a personality badge or guarantee of behavior.

04 / POPULAR LABEL

Physical or somatic empath

Online descriptions use this label for people who notice bodily sensations when someone nearby is unwell or distressed.

Keep in mind: another person’s symptoms cannot be confirmed by intuition; persistent or worrying symptoms deserve medical advice.

05 / POPULAR LABEL

Intuitive or claircognizant empath

Often describes a strong impression about someone’s mood or needs, sometimes framed online as “just knowing.”

Keep in mind: intuition can be a prompt to ask kindly, not proof about someone’s private thoughts or motives.

06 / PARANORMAL CLAIM

Telepathic empath

Some lists use this for a claimed ability to directly sense or receive another person’s thoughts without ordinary communication.

Evidence note: this claimed ability is not established by reliable scientific evidence; check what someone says rather than assuming you know.

07 / PARANORMAL CLAIM

Precognitive empath

Online descriptions attribute to this label a special ability to sense future events or what another person will experience.

Evidence note: predictive or psychic ability is not established; ordinary anticipation is not proof of foreknowledge.

08 / PARANORMAL CLAIM

Psychometric empath

Some popular lists describe this as sensing a person’s history or emotions by touching an object associated with them.

Evidence note: this claimed ability is not research-validated. An object cannot confirm its owner’s feelings or history.

09 / POPULAR LABEL

Animal empath

A label for people who feel especially attuned to animals and attentive to their behavior and welfare.

Keep in mind: caring observation matters, but it does not replace veterinary assessment or careful learning about animal behavior.

10 / POPULAR LABEL

Plant or nature empath

Used for a felt connection with plants, landscapes, or the natural world, and sensitivity to changes in those surroundings.

Keep in mind: people may find this language meaningful; it is not a measured ability to sense plants’ feelings.

11 / POPULAR LABEL

Earth or environmental empath

Describes feeling strongly affected by places, weather, or environmental events, and concern for ecological wellbeing.

Keep in mind: strong reactions to environmental news or sensory surroundings are real experiences, not evidence of a special predictive power.

12 / POPULAR LABEL

Dream empath

Some online lists use this for people who connect dreams with their relationships or emotional life.

Keep in mind: dreams can be personally meaningful, but they are not a reliable way to read another person’s mind or predict events.

13 / CULTURAL CONTEXT MATTERS

“Heyoka empath”

“Heyoka” has specific cultural and spiritual significance in Lakota traditions. “Heyoka empath” is a modern internet label, not a general definition of that tradition.

Respectful context: this site does not speak for Lakota communities or define ceremonial practices. Read our note on the label.

14 / ONE RESEARCH CONSTRUCT

“Dark empath”

A 2021 paper used the term for a self-reported personality profile combining empathy measures with elevated Dark Triad traits in its study sample.

Important: it is not a clinical diagnosis, a way to identify someone, or proof of their intentions. See the study and its limits.

These descriptions summarize common usage, not validated categories. Lists vary between websites, and some labels make claims that have not been established by research. A person’s own account matters more than a label.

THE JOURNAL / SOURCES INCLUDED

Empathy is not
mind-reading.

Short notes on what the research says—and what it does not establish.

01 / NEUROSCIENCE

Mirror neurons: what mirrors, and what doesn’t?

Mirror neurons were first reported in macaque monkeys: some cells responded both when an animal performed a goal-directed action and when it observed a similar action. Researchers have proposed roles in action understanding, but the mechanism and its scope remain debated. Human research often relies on indirect measurements; a brain area that responds during both seeing and doing is not, by itself, proof that a person feels what someone else feels.

Useful distinction: action observation is not the same thing as empathy.

Rizzolatti & Craighero (2004), review ↗ Hickok (2009), critical review ↗

02 / SOCIAL COGNITION

Empathy has more than one component

Empathy research distinguishes processes such as sharing another person’s affect and understanding another person’s perspective. These processes can interact, but they are not identical. A person’s outward reaction alone cannot reliably reveal their private experience or motives.

Useful distinction: noticing emotion, feeling with someone, and reasoning about their perspective are not interchangeable claims.

Decety & Jackson (2004), review ↗

03 / A LABEL, NOT A DIAGNOSIS

What does “dark empath” mean in a paper?

A 2021 study used the term for a research profile combining high reported empathy with elevated “Dark Triad” personality traits. It describes a sample-based personality finding, not a medical diagnosis, validated way to identify someone, or proof that a person is manipulative. A label from one study should not be used to diagnose yourself or others.

Useful distinction: a research construct is not a clinical category.

Heym et al. (2021), original study ↗

04 / CULTURE & INTERNET LANGUAGE

“Heyoka empath”: tradition or internet label?

Heyoka is a Lakota term with cultural and spiritual significance, not a general personality type. Descriptions written outside Lakota communities are not a substitute for Lakota voices; this site cannot define ceremonial practices. “Heyoka empath” is a contemporary internet label sometimes used to describe a person as unusually perceptive or emotionally sensitive. That usage is not a clinical diagnosis or a category established by the empathy research cited here.

Respectful distinction: a modern online label should not be treated as a definition of Lakota belief or identity.

05 / WHAT CAN COEXIST?

Can an “empath” also have dark traits?

In ordinary language, yes: being perceptive or emotionally responsive does not guarantee kindness, honesty, or a particular motive. Research also distinguishes cognitive empathy (inferring another person’s perspective) from affective empathy (sharing or responding to their feelings); these can vary independently. The 2021 “dark empath” paper described a self-report personality profile in its study sample, but it did not study Heyoka identity, Lakota tradition, or a spiritual “Heyoka empath” category.

So there is no research-backed explanation for why a Heyoka would have dark traits—or evidence that being Heyoka causes them. At most, the internet label combines ideas about sensitivity with a separate personality-trait concept. It cannot establish anyone’s character or intentions.

Bottom line: perception is not the same as compassion, and a cultural or spiritual term is not a personality test.

Heym et al. (2021), “The Dark Empath” study ↗ Decety & Jackson (2004), empathy components ↗

These summaries are educational, not personal medical or psychological advice. The linked publications provide the full methods, context, and limitations.
